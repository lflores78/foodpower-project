THE PROJECT

Foodways to Power

Investigating the sociopolitical roots of food inequality and the emergence of “power foods” in the ancient Andes.

01 · OBJECTIVE

Understanding food inequality

FOODPOWER aims to enhance our understanding of the sociopolitical roots of food inequality and to identify foods that became associated with restricted access and emerging elites.

The project investigates how changing foodways became connected to social differentiation and political power as Andean societies developed from early village communities toward increasingly complex political organizations and states.

02 · RESEARCH QUESTIONS

When did food become a marker of inequality?

FOODPOWER asks how access to particular foods changed through time and whether differences in diet can be used to identify emerging patterns of social inequality.

Particular attention is given to foods such as camelid meat and maize, which may have acquired increasing social and political significance as societies became more socially differentiated.

The project investigates whether such foods can be identified as “power foods” — foods associated with restricted access, social status, and emerging forms of political control.

03 · CHRONOLOGY

More than two thousand years of social change

FOODPOWER examines archaeological evidence spanning more than two thousand years of Andean history, from early village societies of the Formative period to increasingly complex societies and states.

This long-term perspective makes it possible to investigate when differences in food access emerged and how they changed alongside broader transformations in social organization and political power.

04 · APPROACH

From archaeological evidence to molecular evidence

FOODPOWER combines archaeological evidence with bioarchaeological approaches to investigate ancient diets and food practices.

The project brings together evidence from human remains and ceramic vessels with bulk and compound-specific isotope analysis, organic residue analysis, proteomics, statistical analysis, and archaeological experiments.

Together, these approaches provide complementary evidence for reconstructing food consumption and investigating its relationship with social differentiation and political power.
