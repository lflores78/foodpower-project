RESEARCH

Food, inequality, and power

FOODPOWER investigates how changing foodways became connected to social differentiation and political power in the ancient Andes.

01 · FOODWAYS AND SOCIAL CHANGE

How did changing foodways transform Andean societies?

FOODPOWER examines long-term changes in foodways, from later hunter-gatherer communities through the adoption and expansion of agriculture and the development of increasingly complex societies.

The project considers how food was produced, prepared, consumed, and distributed, and how these practices changed alongside broader transformations in Andean social organization.

02 · POWER FOODS

When did particular foods become associated with power?

FOODPOWER asks whether particular foods acquired increasing social and political significance as societies became more differentiated.

The project focuses especially on foods such as camelid meat and maize, while remaining open to identifying other resources that may have acquired similar significance through time.

These “power foods” are investigated as foods that may have been associated with restricted access, social status, or emerging forms of political control.

03 · FOOD INEQUALITY

Did access to food become increasingly unequal?

Who had access to particular foods, and how did differences in diet relate to emerging social inequalities?

FOODPOWER investigates differences in food consumption between individuals, groups, and archaeological contexts to identify patterns of dietary differentiation.

These differences are considered in relation to broader processes of social differentiation and changing access to resources.

04 · FOOD AND POLITICAL POWER

How did food become connected to political organization?

FOODPOWER moves beyond the reconstruction of individual diets to investigate how food consumption was related to broader transformations in social and political organization.

The project asks whether changes in access to particular foods accompanied the emergence of increasingly complex societies and states, and whether food became a resource through which inequality and political power were expressed and reproduced.

05 · HOW WE INVESTIGATE FOOD

From archaeological evidence to molecular evidence

FOODPOWER combines archaeological evidence with bioarchaeological and biomolecular approaches to investigate ancient diets and food practices.

The project brings together archaeological contexts, human remains, ceramic vessels, food remains, and archaeological experiments.

These materials are investigated using bulk and compound-specific isotope analysis, organic residue analysis of ceramic vessels, proteomics, and statistical approaches.

Together, these methods provide complementary evidence for reconstructing food consumption and investigating its relationship with social differentiation and political power.

THE CENTRAL QUESTION

FOODPOWER asks not only what people ate, but how food became a resource through which societies expressed, negotiated, and reproduced inequality and power.
